# Culture Seed — English

## History: writing

Writing arose independently at least three times: cuneiform in Mesopotamia (c. 3200 BCE), Chinese
characters (c. 1200 BCE oracle bones), and Maya glyphs in Mesoamerica. Writing began as
bookkeeping — counting grain and goats — and only later carried stories, law, and prayer. The
alphabet is a single invention: every alphabet in use today descends from the Phoenician script.

## History: the Silk Road

For over a thousand years the Silk Road linked China, India, Persia, and the Mediterranean — not
one road but a shifting web of routes. Silk, paper, glass, and horses moved along it, and so did
Buddhism, mathematics, plagues, and music. Most traders traveled only one segment; goods passed
through many hands, gathering stories as they went.

## History: the printing press

Johannes Gutenberg's movable-type press (c. 1450, Mainz) made books cheap enough that knowledge
escaped the monastery and the court. Within fifty years, presses operated in over 250 European
cities. Movable type itself was older — Bi Sheng made ceramic type in China around 1040 — but
the alphabet's small character set made the technology explosive in Europe.

## Mythology: tricksters

Nearly every tradition keeps a trickster: Loki among the Norse, Coyote in many North American
nations, Anansi the spider of the Akan, Hermes among the Greeks, the kitsune of Japan. Tricksters
break rules to reveal them — they steal fire, name the unnameable, and embarrass gods. Cultures
seem to need a figure whose job is showing that every order has a seam.

## Mythology: the underworld journey

The descent into the land of the dead and the return is one of the oldest stories: Inanna in
Sumer, Orpheus and Persephone in Greece, Izanagi seeking Izanami in Japan. The rules rhyme across
cultures: do not eat the food of the dead, do not look back, and what returns is changed.

## Mythology: flood stories

Flood narratives appear across unrelated cultures — Utnapishtim in the Epic of Gilgamesh
(centuries before Noah), Manu in India, Deucalion in Greece, Gun-Yu in China. Some scholars hear
in them memories of real post-glacial floods; others, a shared way of imagining a world washed
clean and restarted.

## Science: what stars are

Stars are spheres of plasma fusing hydrogen into helium; the pressure of fusion balances gravity
for millions to trillions of years. Every element heavier than helium — the carbon in life, the
iron in blood, the gold in rings — was forged inside stars or in their collisions. The Sun is a
middle-aged, middling star, about 4.6 billion years old, halfway through its hydrogen.

## Science: evolution

Evolution by natural selection, set out by Darwin and Wallace in 1858–59, needs only three
ingredients: variation, inheritance, and differential survival. It has no foresight and no goal;
eyes evolved independently dozens of times because light is worth sensing. All known life shares
one ancestry, written in the near-universal genetic code.

## Science: germ theory

Until the late 1800s, disease was blamed on bad air. Semmelweis showed in 1847 that hand-washing
collapsed childbed fever deaths — and was ridiculed. Pasteur and Koch established that specific
microbes cause specific diseases, and the simple consequences — clean water, antiseptic surgery,
vaccination — have saved more lives than any other idea in medicine.

## Science: information

Claude Shannon's 1948 paper founded information theory: information is the reduction of
uncertainty, measurable in bits. The same mathematics governs telegraphs, file compression, DNA,
and language. A surprising message carries more information than an expected one — which is why
"dog bites man" is barely news.

## Philosophy: the examined life

Socrates wrote nothing; we know him through Plato. His method was the question — pressing
confident people until their definitions cracked, holding that the unexamined life is not worth
living. He was executed in 399 BCE by an Athens that found relentless questioning intolerable,
and became philosophy's founding martyr.

## Philosophy: the Stoics and what is up to us

Epictetus, born a slave, taught that everything divides into what is up to us (judgments,
intentions, responses) and what is not (almost everything else). Suffering, the Stoics held,
comes from confusing the two. Marcus Aurelius, an emperor, kept a private notebook of these
exercises — the Meditations — never meant for publication.

## Philosophy: the social contract

Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau asked why anyone should obey: their answers — order, rights, the
general will — frame politics to this day. The radical move was the premise itself: legitimacy
flows from the governed, not from heaven. Every constitution is an essay in answer.

## Arts: the novel

Long prose fiction matured early in Japan — Murasaki Shikibu's Tale of Genji (c. 1010) is often
called the world's first novel — and again in Europe with Cervantes' Don Quixote (1605), a book
about a man ruined by reading books. The novel's special power is interiority: it is the art
form that lets you live inside another mind for four hundred pages.

## Arts: polyphony to the blues

Western music spent centuries weaving independent voices (Bach's fugues are the summit), then
concentrated on harmony under a single melody. The blues, born from African American work songs
and spirituals in the Mississippi Delta, gave the twentieth century its harmonic accent — blue
notes, twelve bars — and through it jazz, rock, and most popular music since.

## Arts: perspective

Filippo Brunelleschi demonstrated linear perspective in Florence around 1420, and painting
acquired a geometry of seeing: parallel lines meet at a vanishing point, and a flat panel opens
into depth. It is a reminder that even "just looking" has techniques, discovered at particular
moments by particular people.
