The lighthouse keeper climbed the spiral stairs each evening at dusk, trimmed
the wick, and logged the weather in a canvas-bound ledger. Ships passing the
headland saw the beam sweep the water every seven seconds, as it had for forty
years. On clear nights the fishing fleet used it to time their return, and the
keeper marked each passage with a small tick in the margin.
A river begins as meltwater high in the ranges, threading between stones too
small to name. By the valley floor it has opinions: it undercuts banks, carries
silt, rearranges its own bed each spring. Towns grow at the crossings and take
the river's temper as their calendar.
The bakery opened at five, long before the street had colors. Flour, steam on
the windows, the radio low. By seven the racks were full and the first
customers came in with cold hands, buying bread they would eat while walking.
To repot a fern, water it the day before, then turn the pot on its side and
ease the root ball free. Loosen the outer roots with your fingers, set it into
fresh soil at the same depth it grew before, and press gently — roots want
contact, not compaction. Water once, then leave it alone in bright shade.
The train slowed through the outskirts, past allotments and rusted goalposts,
and the man across the aisle folded his newspaper into smaller and smaller
squares until it fit his coat pocket exactly.
